What is the vertical distance h of P above Q?

3 Step-by-Step Guided Solution

Values below update live with your sliders. Click a step to expand it, then reveal its hint.

Choose the zero of gravitational potential energy at the table-top height (through Q). Since the particle starts at rest at P, it has only potential energy there. At Q the height is zero, so all that potential energy has become kinetic:

mgh = 12mv²  ⇒  v = √(2gh)
Live:  v = √(2 × 9.81 × 1.125) = 4.70 m/s

Note the mass cancels and the curve's shape is irrelevant — only the vertical drop h matters.

Formulae: K.E. = ½mv², gravitational P.E. = mgy (with y measured up). Released from rest ⇒ K.E.P = 0. The curve does no work because the normal reaction is always perpendicular to the velocity (frictionless, smooth).
Conceptual check: if the release height h were doubled, the speed at Q would grow by a factor √2 — speed grows only as the square root of drop height.

Once the particle leaves the table it moves under gravity alone. The launch at Q is purely horizontal, so the initial vertical velocity is zero. The vertical motion is therefore free fall from the table height H:

H = 12gt²  ⇒  t = √ 2H g
Live:  t = √(2 × 2.00 / 9.81) = 0.639 s
Formulae: s = ut + ½at². Vertically u = 0, a = g, s = H ⇒ H = ½gt².
Key insight: horizontal and vertical motions are independent. The fall time depends only on H and g — it does not depend on how fast the particle is moving horizontally.

Horizontally there is no force, so the particle keeps its launch speed v the whole way down. Substitute v = √(2gh) and t = √(2H/g):

x = v·t = √(2gh) · √ 2H g  = 2√(hH)  ⇒  x² = 4hH
Live:  x = 2√(1.125 × 2.00) = 3.00 m
Why it works: √(2gh) × √(2H/g) = √(4hH) = 2√(hH). The g cancels completely — a great shortcut. The result x² = 4hH is the whole problem in one line.
Try it: change h on the simulator. The predicted range follows x = 2√(hH) exactly. When h = 1.125 m and H = 2 m you should get x = 3.00 m — that is what makes this the answer.
h = x²4H  =  (3 m)²4 × 2 m  =  98  =  1.125 m
Live (your sliders):  h = 1.125 m is needed to hit the target at x = 3.00 m
Units: x² / 4H = (m²)/(m) = m — the answer comes out in metres. Always sanity-check units!
Sanity check: 1.125 m is comfortably less than the 2 m table, so the launch speed is modest (≈ 4.7 m/s) and the 0.64 s fall carries it exactly 3 m. Sensible.
h ≈ 1.13 m  →  Answer: A
Options B (1.33 m) comes from a careless √(3²/(2·2)) error; C and D ignore energy conservation or the quadratic time-of-flight relation. Only A matches x² = 4hH.
4 Formula Breakdown & Key Hints
Energy on the curve
½mv² = mgh  ⇒  v = √(2gh)
  • Applies because the curve is smooth (frictionless).
  • Shape and length of the curve are irrelevant.
  • Particle released from rest: initial kinetic energy = 0.
Projectile time of flight
H = ½gt²  ⇒  t = √(2H/g)
  • Launch is horizontal: initial vy = 0.
  • Fall time depends only on table height H, not on speed.
  • Horizontal & vertical motions are independent.
The golden shortcut
x = v·t = 2√(hH)  ⇒  x² = 4hH
  • The gravitational constant g cancels — beautiful!
  • Range ∝ √h: double h → range × √2.
  • Use it to flip the problem: h = x²/(4H).
Common traps
  • Forgetting energy conservation and using projectile kinematics on the curve — wrong.
  • Using vertical speed at Q (it is zero!) in the range formula.
  • Mixing up H (table height, 2 m) with h (release height, unknown).
  • Rounding 1.125 to 1.3 or 1.1 before comparing options — keep 1.13.
